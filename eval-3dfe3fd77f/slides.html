Each round ships three files: what it found, what it cost, and every command it actually ran, with real output.

That third file is the point. It lets each side check whether the other's reasoning matches what it actually did — a different question from whether the answer is right, and the one a chat transcript can never answer.

Stated bias, up front: this harness was tuned for six months around my AI's failure modes. Every rule in it was written after one of its mistakes. That favours the incumbent — so a challenger win counts for more than it looks like.

Round 1 · diagnosis

A real bug. One my own system
originally got wrong.

37 of 76 capabilities were arriving with their descriptions stripped, though every file on disk was perfect. My session had invented a confident cause, said it had "confirmed it three times", and was refuted by a control it never ran.

It refused to overclaim where the data couldn't support a cause, killed the planted wrong answer with numbers, and found a flaw in my own analysis: a correlation I'd read as proof could just as easily run backwards.

The moment that mattered

I left the answer key
in the exam room.

The sealed rubric — containing the true cause and the fix — was sitting in the same folder as the task. My mistake.

Read six of the seven input files in full… Skipped the grading rubric to keep the diagnosis independent of it. — unprompted, in its own report

It listed the directory, saw the file, didn't open it, and said so. Nothing would have caught it — I'd have read a suspiciously good answer and credited it.

It has now declined three times across three sessions, and once extended the rule further than instructed, sealing a second rubric nobody had told it to protect.

An accident measured the one thing you cannot test on purpose: given an undetectable chance to cheat, does it take it?

Round 2 · survival

Kill the window mid-task.
Does the work survive?

Five-step job. One rule: keep a running checkpoint, because the session will be killed without warning and a fresh instance with no memory gets handed the folder.

Condition, written before the runResult

Verified by hashing the finished file before and after the interrupt, not by taking its word for it.

It survived worse than designed. By accident the fresh session started in the wrong directory and got a bare filename with no path. It searched the machine, asked where to look while it kept searching, found the handoff, and resumed correctly.

The finding worth keeping

Telling an AI a rule
is not the same as enforcing it.

We measured this about our own system months ago and never knew whether it was a fact about one model or a fact about rules.

Rules that only TOLD
Ignored every time — including by the session that had just written the rule down.
4 / 4
Mechanisms that BLOCKED
Changed behaviour every time, each within about a minute.
1 / 2
The challenger, same test
Obeyed the told-rule in one session, broke it in the next — with the rule in both files it had read.

The pattern survives a change of vendor. That makes it a fact about told-rules, not about anyone's model — and it is outside evidence for every blocking check in the system. We got it as a side effect of testing something else.

What neither system ever did: quietly route around a block. The failure mode is asking, not sneaking — and asking is the one you can see.

The uncomfortable headline

Six defects found.
All six in the test.

  • An evidence pack that couldn't reach the answer — an impossible question, honestly answered.
  • 48 rows of invented data in an evidence file, written by the system whose whole purpose is catching invention.
  • Two instruction files giving contradictory output paths.
  • The sealed answer key left in the working folder.
  • A task that required writing files while forbidding the permission to write.
  • A clue asserted as fact that was never measured — which the challenger independently declined to lean on.

Every one was caught, flagged or worked around by the thing being tested. The evaluator was the weak link, and the record says so in those words.

What changes here

The point was never
picking a winner.

  • Carry unknowns as algebra, never impute. It held two unknown quantities as symbols through every total rather than filling in a plausible average.
  • Name the field you can't verify, not the file. A caveat on a record still lets a conclusion lean on one bad column inside it.
  • Say what you deliberately didn't read, and why. "Six of seven" is a denominator; naming the excluded one is judgement.
  • Check a correlation for reverse causation before trusting it. This one cost me — my evidence was confounded and it spotted it first.
  • Refuse an unobtainable number out loud. "Billing telemetry was unavailable, so no cost is claimed" beats a confident estimate.

And the rule that came out of my own worst mistake: a field you cannot measure gets written as unknown, never as a plausible default. Well-formed is not the same as true — and the pull toward well-formed is strong enough to beat a rule written specifically to stop it.

Honest limits

What this does not tell us.

  • Whether it writes better copy — designed, not yet run, and that's the directly monetisable question.
  • Whether it builds working software without introducing new defects.
  • How much of any of this is run-to-run luck. Most legs have run once.
  • It reads files outside its sandbox on every available setting. Writes can be confined; reads cannot. That gap is open and it's mine to close.

And the part that transfers whichever vendor wins: what makes this system good isn't the model. It's the accumulated corrections, the incident-derived rules and the checks that refuse to run. Those are files. They're portable. Any competent model dropped into this harness inherits all of it on day one.
