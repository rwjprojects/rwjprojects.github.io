Which Sunglasses
for Which Sky

You asked whether to get polarized, and what color. Short answer: gray or brown, normal dark, not polarized. Here's why, and what the evidence actually says.

Jump to: Order a pair By sky Lens colors

1. Darkness matters more than color.

Wear normal dark sunglasses, gray or brown. Skip the very dark "glacier" kind: the FAA warns that lenses blocking more than 85% of light can cost you sharpness.

2. Color: gray or brown. Both work.

Gray keeps colors true and is the FAA's first pick. Brown or copper is what many pilots, hawk-watchers and clay shooters reach for, but in a lab test it did no better than gray. Pick the one you like. Blue lenses do nothing for you.

3. Don't pay extra for polarized.

Polarized lenses kill glare off water and roads. The sky isn't water. They can black out your transmitter screen, and the FAA says they hide the glint off a wing.

4. Gray day? Light yellow, or no glasses.

In lab tests on a screen, yellow and orange lenses made bright stripes on blue stand out more; nobody has tried that with planes. Yellow also throws colors off and makes everything look brighter than it is. Save it for dull days, as a cheap second pair.

If you just want to order a pair

  1. Look for "100% UV protection" or "UV400". Usually free, and nothing to do with darkness. Never wear dark lenses without it.
  2. Say no to polarized.
  3. Color: gray, or brown/copper if you prefer it. Not blue.
  4. Darkness: "Dark". If the site asks for a tint percentage instead, pick 80: the lens blocks 80% of the light, which is normal sunglasses dark. Anything from 70 to 85 is fine; the FAA's sunglasses guide says don't go darker than 85. If the label shows only a category, a dark category 2 or a light category 3 is right.
  5. Use your current distance prescription, from an exam in the last couple of years.
  6. Pick polycarbonate lenses with a scratch-resistant coating. They're the most impact-resistant kind, and props and crashes throw things. With a strong prescription, ask your optician about color fringing when you look off to the side.
  7. A big wrap-around lens, or a frame that hugs your face, to keep wind off your eyes.
  8. Optional second pair for gray days: light yellow, the "Light" option.
  9. Wear a cap with a brim. Pull it down against a low sun; the FDA and eye doctors recommend a brim too.
  10. Sunglasses aren't safety glasses. All US sunglasses must pass a basic FDA impact test, but near a running prop, safety glasses rated for high impact (usually marked "Z87+") protect more. If you need a prescription, ask your optician for prescription safety glasses.

How dark is "dark"?

Three strengths. Stores label them different ways; the checklist above covers the common ones. In plain terms:

Dark

Everyday sunglasses. The pair you'd drive with at noon in July. Blocks about 70–85% of the light (the "80% tint" on most order pages). Sunny days. Your main pair.

Medium

Noticeably lighter; people can still see your eyes. Nice for hazy or partly cloudy days, but Dark works there too.

Light

Barely tinted, like yellow shooting glasses. Gray, overcast days.

The FAA's reason for its 85% limit: darker lenses can make instruments and printed material hard to read. Your transmitter is your instrument: if you can't read it through them in the shade, they're too dark.

Polarized, in plain English

What it's for: glare bouncing off flat things. Water, wet roads, a car hood.

The sky isn't a lake. Blue sky is only strongly polarized in one curved stripe, a quarter-turn away from the sun (see the picture). A polarized lens darkens that stripe and leaves the rest of the sky alone. So the plane stands out a bit more in one part of the sky, no more anywhere else, and the sky behind it changes brightness as it crosses. Clouds wipe the effect out and haze weakens it, so under overcast a polarized lens is just a dark lens.

Two real downsides. Tilt your head and it blacks out screens: your transmitter, your phone. And it dims the sun flash off a wing, which many of us use to tell which way the plane is pointing. The FAA tells full-scale pilots not to wear polarized for those reasons.

10-second test: are my sunglasses polarized?

Look at your phone screen through one lens and turn the phone slowly sideways. If the screen goes dark at one angle and comes back at another, they're polarized. Your transmitter screen will do the same.

Try it with your own plane

Your plane's colors matter too: a tint that makes one color scheme pop can wash out another, so what works for a buddy's plane may not work for yours. We found no test of lenses on model planes, so before you buy a second pair, fly the same plane on the same day with each pair you own, and with none. Swap pairs on the ground between flights, or have a buddy fly while you watch. Notice three things: how soon you pick the plane up, whether you can tell which way it's pointing, and whether you can still read your transmitter.

What other pilots say

About fifteen forum threads and magazine pieces, none a proper side-by-side test. What came up repeatedly: polarized blanks the transmitter screen (everyone has a workaround); pilots are split on whether polarized helps or hurts orientation (four said hurts, two said helps); brown or amber is the favorite color; and cheap sunglasses blur the plane, so several pilots fly without.

Where this comes from (13 sources; checked 21 Aug 2026; the September changes checked at the source 17 Sept 2026)
  • FAA, "Sunglasses for Pilots: Beyond the Image" (OK-24-1154, Sept 2024): gray recommended; gray, gray-green and brown all "an excellent choice"; yellow/amber "no scientific studies support" the sharper-vision claim; polarized "not recommended"; block "only 70 - 85%" of light, and lenses blocking more than 85% not recommended; photochromic lenses "not recommended" (they clear slowly and darken poorly above 70°F); polycarbonate is "the most impact-resistant" lens but has more built-in color fringing (Abbe value 31, against 58 for standard CR-39 plastic). faa.gov
  • AOPA: traffic between you and a low sun is "very difficult to see."
  • Lab study, 2000 (Wolffsohn, Optometry & Vision Science): yellow lenses improved contrast of bright objects "against a blue-based background, such as the sky," at a cost to color vision. Brown was not tested.
  • Lab study, 2002 (de Fez): brown, green and blue lenses no different from gray for contrast, though they still disturbed color judgment; yellow/orange slightly better for contrast, worse for colors.
  • Lens materials (Doroslovački & Guyton, J AAPOS 2015): in prescription lenses, polycarbonate showed more color fringing off-center than CR-39, while CR-39 showed more of a different off-center blur. Our inference: plain lenses bend light too little for either to matter much.
  • Safety eyewear: ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2025 is the current US standard (ISEA, Jan 2026); "Z87+ is rated for high impact" (UCLA safety office). It is a workplace standard, not a sunglass standard.
  • Two lab studies (Kelly, 1984 and 1990): people who had a preference chose yellow 2-to-1, and large scenes look up to 40% brighter through it, but they didn't actually see finer detail.
  • US Air Force field test (Aerospace Medical Research Lab, 1971): ten observers searching for ground targets in moderate haze; yellow sunglasses "of no value" for spotting them, and slightly worse for identifying them.
  • Physics papers (Applied Optics 2003, 2008): sky polarization peaks a quarter-turn from the sun; light from clouds is effectively unpolarized, and haze weakens the effect.
  • American Academy of Ophthalmology: brown/rose give extra contrast; avoid polarized with screens; "polarization itself does not provide UV protection".
  • FDA: choose "a UV400 rating or "100% UV protection""; "up to 80% of the sun's UV rays can get through the clouds". AAO: US sunglasses "must meet Food and Drug Administration impact safety standards"; get polycarbonate with "a scratch-resistant coating".
  • Zenni Optical: "dark (80% tint)" means 80% of light blocked, which is where the checklist's number comes from.
  • Pilot forums and press: HeliFreak, RC Universe, RCGroups, HobbySquawk, Model Aviation (Aug 2024), a soaring blog. RC Universe quotes came from search snippets; the threads themselves are behind a block.
