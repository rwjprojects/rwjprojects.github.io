Field handout for RC pilots · August 2026

Which Sunglasses
for Which Sky

We got talking at the field about polarized lenses, lens colors, and why the plane sometimes goes blurry. Here's what's proven, what's likely, and what's just the salesman talking. Plain English.

1. How dark matters more than what color.

Wear normal dark sunglasses, gray or brown. Skip the super-dark "glacier" kind: past a point, darker means less detail. That's the one rule the FAA and the eye doctors agree on.

2. Brown or copper is our pick for a plane on blue sky.

Brown cuts the blue haze, and a light-colored plane may stand out a little more. Gray is just as good for most people. Blue lenses do nothing for you.

3. Don't pay extra for polarized.

Polarized is for glare off water and roads. Against the sky it darkens only one stripe of blue. It can black out your transmitter screen, and the FAA says it hides the glint off a wing.

4. Cloudy day? Light yellow, or no glasses.

A light yellow pair makes a gray day feel brighter. It won't make the plane any sharper, but it's comfortable and cheap.

5. "Blink and it clears up" is dry eyes, not your glasses.

Staring up into wind and sun dries your eyes out. Eye drops, a wrap-around frame and a hat brim fix more than any lens color will. See "The plane goes blurry" below.

How dark is "dark"?

Sunglasses come in roughly three strengths. Stores label them "category 3" or "80% tint". You only need that number if you order online (see the checklist). Think of it like this:

Dark

Everyday sunglasses. The pair you'd drive with at noon in July. Sunny days.

Medium

Noticeably lighter. People can still see your eyes through them. Partly cloudy and hazy-bright days.

Light

Barely tinted, like yellow shooting glasses or safety glasses. Gray, overcast days.

The FAA's rule of thumb: dark enough that you're not squinting, never so dark that things lose detail. If you can't read your transmitter through them in the shade, they're too dark.

Polarized, in plain English

What it's for: glare bouncing off flat things. Water, wet roads, a car hood. Fishermen love it for good reason.

The sky isn't a lake. Blue sky only has that kind of glare in one curved stripe, a quarter-turn away from the sun. Polarized lenses darken that stripe and leave the rest alone. So the plane stands out a bit more in one part of the sky and no more anywhere else. And as it crosses, the sky behind it changes brightness. Photographers use the same trick and complain about the "ugly bands" it makes.

Clouds and haze turn it off. Under overcast, a polarized lens is just a dark lens you paid extra for.

Two real downsides. Tilt your head and it blacks out screens: your transmitter, your phone. And it dims the sun flash off a wing, which a lot of us use to tell which way the plane is pointing. The FAA tells full-scale pilots not to wear them for exactly those reasons.

10-second test: are my sunglasses polarized?

Hold your phone up with the screen on and look at it through one lens. Turn the phone slowly sideways. If the screen goes dark at one angle and comes back at another, they're polarized. That's what will happen to your transmitter screen, too.

Ordering a pair online (EyeBuyDirect, Zenni, anywhere)

  1. Tick "UV protection" (sometimes called UV400). Usually free. Nothing to do with how dark the lens is; even clear lenses can have it. Never wear dark lenses without it.
  2. Say no to polarized. Save the money.
  3. Color: brown or copper. Gray if you'd rather have true colors. Not blue.
  4. Darkness: "Dark". If the site asks for a tint percentage instead, pick 80. That's the normal dark strength (some sites just say Dark / Medium / Light).
  5. Use your current distance prescription, from an exam in the last couple of years.
  6. Pick polycarbonate (impact-resistant) lenses. Props, crashes and debris: you want lenses that don't shatter.
  7. A big wrap-around lens, or a frame that hugs your face. Keeps wind off your eyes.
  8. Optional cheap second pair for gray days: light yellow, the "Light" option (not dark).
  9. In the flight box: eye drops and a ball cap.

What other pilots say

We read about fifteen forum threads and magazine pieces (most through search snippets, because the big RC forums block automated reading). Nobody has posted a proper side-by-side test. A few things came up repeatedly:

  • Polarized blanks the transmitter screen. Everyone who wears them has a workaround: tilt the transmitter, or pop the glasses off.
  • Split on whether polarized helps you tell which way the plane's pointing. Four pilots said it hurts; two said it helps.
  • Brown or amber is the favorite color in most of the threads we read. One pilot keeps two pairs: gray when the sun's in front of him, brown when it's behind.
  • Cheap sunglasses blur the plane. Several pilots take them off and squint instead. That's lens quality, not color.

Where this comes from

Every claim on this page was checked against its source a second time on 21 August 2026. The full write-up with every quote: rwjprojects.github.io/sunglasses/detailed.html. The short list:

  • FAA, "Sunglasses for Pilots: Beyond the Image" (OK-24-1154, Sept 2024): gray recommended; gray, gray-green and brown all "an excellent choice"; yellow/amber "no scientific studies support" the sharper-vision claim; polarized "not recommended"; lenses blocking more than 85% of light not recommended. faa.gov
  • FAA flight manual (AIM 8-1-6): "empty-field myopia": with nothing to look at, the eyes drift to a focus of 10 to 30 feet.
  • AOPA: traffic between you and a low sun is "very difficult to see."
  • Lab study, 2000 (Wolffsohn, Optometry & Vision Science): yellow lenses improved contrast of bright objects "against a blue-based background, such as the sky," at a cost to color vision. Brown was not tested.
  • Lab study, 2002 (de Fez): brown, green and blue lenses no different from gray for contrast; yellow/orange slightly better, worse for colors.
  • Two lab studies (Kelly, 1984 and 1990): people who had a preference chose yellow 2-to-1, and large scenes look up to 40% brighter through it, but they didn't actually see finer detail.
  • US Air Force field test (Aerospace Medical Research Lab, 1971): ten observers searching for ground targets in moderate haze; yellow sunglasses "of no value" for spotting them, and slightly worse for identifying them.
  • Physics papers (Applied Optics 2003, 2008): sky polarization peaks a quarter-turn from the sun; clouds and haze wipe it out.
  • American Academy of Ophthalmology: brown/rose give extra contrast; avoid polarized with screens; "polarization itself does not provide UV protection"; wrap-around glasses for dry eye; cataract clouding common after 60; exams every 1–3 years at 55–64 for people without other eye problems.
  • National Eye Institute and Mayo Clinic: dry eye causes blurry vision; artificial tears for mild cases; wraparound sunglasses outside; looking up opens the eyes wider so tears evaporate faster.
  • Zenni Optical: "dark (80% tint)" means 80% of light blocked, which is where the checklist's number comes from.
  • Pilot forums and press: HeliFreak, RC Universe, RCGroups, HobbySquawk, Model Aviation (Aug 2024), a soaring blog: about fifteen threads and articles. RC Universe quotes came from search snippets; we could not open the threads themselves.

Honest note: nobody has ever tested a small model plane against the sky through different lenses. Everything here is physics, lab tests and experienced people's advice, applied to what we do. Good advice, not a guarantee.
