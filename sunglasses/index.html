Field handout for RC pilots · August 2026

Which Sunglasses
for Which Sky

We got talking at the field about polarized lenses, lens colors and why the plane sometimes goes blurry. Here's what's actually proven, what's likely, and what's just the salesman talking — in plain English.

1. How dark matters more than what color.

Normal dark sunglasses in gray or brown. Not the super-dark "glacier" kind — too dark and you lose sharpness. That's the one rule the FAA and the eye doctors agree on.

2. Brown or copper is the best color for a plane on blue sky.

Brown cuts the blue haze and makes a light plane stand out a little more against the sky. Gray is fine too. Blue lenses do nothing for you.

3. Don't pay extra for polarized.

Polarized is for glare off water and roads. Against the sky it only darkens one stripe of blue, it can black out your transmitter screen, and the FAA says it hides the glint off a wing.

4. Cloudy day? Light yellow or no glasses.

A light yellow or amber pair makes a gray day feel brighter. It won't make the plane sharper — but it's comfortable and cheap.

5. "Blink and it clears up" is dry eyes, not your glasses.

Staring up into wind and sun dries your eyes out. Eye drops, a wrap-around frame and a hat brim fix more than any lens color will.

First, the only jargon you need: how dark is "dark"?

Sunglasses come in roughly three strengths. Stores call them "categories" or "80% tint" — ignore that. Think of it like this:

Dark

Everyday sunglasses. The pair you'd drive with at noon in July. Use these for sunny days.

Medium

Noticeably lighter. People can still see your eyes through them. Partly cloudy, hazy bright days.

Light

Barely tinted — like yellow shooting glasses or safety glasses. Gray, overcast days.

Rule of thumb from the FAA: dark enough that you're not squinting, but never so dark that things lose detail. If you can't read your transmitter through them in the shade, they're too dark.

Polarized, in plain English

What it's for: glare bouncing off flat things — water, wet roads, a car hood. Fishermen love it for good reason.

The sky isn't a lake. Blue sky is only "glare-like" in one curved stripe, 90° away from the sun. Polarized lenses darken that stripe and leave the rest alone. So your plane gets a bit more contrast in one part of the sky and none in the rest — and the sky changes brightness under it as it crosses. Photographers use the same trick and complain about the "ugly bands" it makes.

Clouds and haze turn it off. Under overcast, a polarized lens is just a dark lens you paid extra for.

The two real downsides: at certain head angles it blacks out LCD screens — your transmitter, your phone — and it dims the sun flash off a wing that a lot of us use to catch which way the plane is pointing. The FAA tells full-scale pilots not to wear them for exactly those reasons.

10-second test: are my sunglasses polarized?

Hold your phone up with the screen on and look at it through the lenses. Now turn the phone sideways. If the screen goes dark or black, they're polarized. That's also exactly what will happen to your transmitter screen at the wrong angle.

What other pilots say

We read 20 forum threads and magazine pieces. Nobody's done a proper side-by-side test, but a few things came up over and over:

  • Polarized blanks the transmitter screen. Everyone who wears them has a workaround — tilt the radio, or pop the glasses off.
  • Split on polarized and orientation. Some say the plane "stands out more." Just as many say it hurts depth and orientation because "the reflections actually help your brain."
  • Brown/amber is the favorite color, by a wide margin. One guy keeps two pairs: gray when the sun's in front of him, brown when it's behind.
  • Cheap sunglasses blur the plane. Several pilots take them off and squint instead. That's lens quality, not color.
  • Gray lenses on a yellow plane — one pilot couldn't see his buddy's yellow sailplane through gray at all; the buddy wore orange lenses.
"I wear amber lens sunglasses over my regular glasses when flying. They enhance contrast and really make colors pop against the blue skies."RC Universe pattern-flying forum

Ordering a pair online (EyeBuyDirect, Zenni, anywhere)

  1. Tick "UV protection" / UV400. Usually free. Nothing to do with how dark the lens is — even clear lenses can have it.
  2. Say no to polarized. Save the money.
  3. Color: brown or copper. Gray if you'd rather have true colors. Not blue.
  4. Darkness: "Dark" — the normal sunglasses strength. If the site asks for a tint percentage, 80% is normal dark. Don't go past 85%.
  5. Your current distance prescription, from an exam in the last couple of years.
  6. A big, wrapped lens or a frame that hugs your face. Keeps wind off your eyes.
  7. Optional cheap second pair for gray days: light yellow or amber, "Light" strength (30–50% tint if they ask).
  8. In the flight box: eye drops and a ball cap.

Where this comes from

The full write-up with every quote: detailed.html. The short list:

  • FAA, "Sunglasses for Pilots: Beyond the Image" (2024) — gray recommended; brown and gray-green "excellent"; yellow/amber "no scientific studies support" the sharper-vision claim; polarized "not recommended"; don't block more than 85% of light. faa.gov
  • FAA flight manual (AIM 8-1-6) — "empty-field myopia": eyes drift to a focus of 10–30 feet when there's nothing to look at.
  • AOPA — traffic between you and a low sun is "very difficult to see."
  • Lab study, 2000 (Wolffsohn, Optometry & Vision Science) — blue-cutting tints improved contrast of bright objects "against a blue-based background, such as the sky," at a cost to color vision.
  • Lab study, 2002 (de Fez) — brown, green and blue lenses no different from gray for contrast; yellow/orange slightly better but worse for colors.
  • Two lab studies (Kelly, 1984 & 1990) — people prefer yellow 2-to-1 and it looks up to 40% brighter, but they don't actually see finer detail.
  • US Army field test — yellow sunglasses "of no value" for spotting targets from the air in haze.
  • Physics papers (Applied Optics 2003, 2008) — sky polarization peaks 90° from the sun; clouds and haze wipe it out.
  • American Academy of Ophthalmology — brown/rose give extra contrast; avoid polarized with LCD screens; "polarization itself does not provide UV protection"; wrap-around glasses for dry eye; cataract clouding common after 60.
  • National Eye Institute — dry eye: blurry vision, artificial tears; wear wraparound sunglasses outside.
  • Pilot forums — RCGroups, RC Universe, HeliFreak, HobbySquawk, Model Aviation (Aug 2024), a soaring blog: 20 threads/articles.

Honest note: nobody has ever tested a small model plane against the sky through different lenses. Everything here is physics, lab tests and experienced people's advice, applied to what we do. Good advice — not a guarantee.
