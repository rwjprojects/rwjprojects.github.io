Which Sunglasses
for Which Sky

You asked whether to get polarized, and what color. Short answer: brown or gray, normal dark, not polarized. Here's why, and what the evidence actually says.

Jump to: Order a pair By sky Lens colors

1. Darkness matters more than color.

Wear normal dark sunglasses, gray or brown. Skip the very dark "glacier" kind: past a point, darker means less detail. The FAA and the eye doctors agree on this one.

2. Color: brown or copper. Gray is fine too.

Brown trims the blue haze and is what pilots, hawk-watchers and clay shooters reach for. Gray keeps colors true. Either works. Blue lenses do nothing for you.

3. Don't pay extra for polarized.

Polarized lenses kill glare off water and roads. The sky isn't water. They can black out your transmitter screen, and the FAA says they hide the glint off a wing.

4. Gray day? Light yellow, or no glasses.

Yellow is the one color a lab test showed helps a bright object stand out against blue sky, but it throws colors off and makes everything look brighter than it is. Save it for dull days, as a cheap second pair.

If you just want to order a pair

  1. Tick "UV protection" (sometimes called UV400). Usually free, and nothing to do with darkness. Never wear dark lenses without it.
  2. Say no to polarized.
  3. Color: brown or copper. Gray if you prefer true colors. Not blue.
  4. Darkness: "Dark". If the site asks for a tint percentage instead, pick 80. On those sites, 80% means the lens blocks 80% of the light, which is normal sunglasses dark.
  5. Use your current distance prescription, from an exam in the last couple of years.
  6. Pick polycarbonate lenses, the shatter-resistant plastic. Props and crashes throw things.
  7. A big wrap-around lens, or a frame that hugs your face, to keep wind off your eyes.
  8. Optional second pair for gray days: light yellow, the "Light" option.
  9. Wear a ball cap. When the sun is low, a brim does more than any lens.

How dark is "dark"?

Three strengths. Stores call them things like "category 3" or "80% tint"; the checklist above covers that. In plain terms:

Dark

Everyday sunglasses. The pair you'd drive with at noon in July. Sunny days. Your main pair.

Medium

Noticeably lighter; people can still see your eyes. Nice for hazy or partly cloudy days, but Dark works there too.

Light

Barely tinted, like yellow shooting glasses. Gray, overcast days.

The FAA's rule of thumb: dark enough that you're not squinting, never so dark that things lose detail. If you can't read your transmitter through them in the shade, they're too dark.

Polarized, in plain English

What it's for: glare bouncing off flat things. Water, wet roads, a car hood.

The sky isn't a lake. Blue sky only has that kind of glare in one curved stripe, a quarter-turn away from the sun (see the picture). A polarized lens darkens that stripe and leaves the rest of the sky alone. So the plane stands out a bit more in one part of the sky, no more anywhere else, and the sky behind it changes brightness as it crosses. Clouds and haze scramble the effect completely, so under overcast a polarized lens is just a dark lens.

Two real downsides. Tilt your head and it blacks out screens: your transmitter, your phone. And it dims the sun flash off a wing, which many of us use to tell which way the plane is pointing. The FAA tells full-scale pilots not to wear polarized for those reasons.

10-second test: are my sunglasses polarized?

Look at your phone screen through one lens and turn the phone slowly sideways. If the screen goes dark at one angle and comes back at another, they're polarized. Your transmitter screen will do the same.

What other pilots say

About fifteen forum threads and magazine pieces, none a proper side-by-side test. What came up repeatedly: polarized blanks the transmitter screen (everyone has a workaround); pilots are split on whether polarized helps or hurts orientation (four said hurts, two said helps); brown or amber is the favorite color; and cheap sunglasses blur the plane, so several pilots fly without.

"They are both non-polarized lenses. … the Corning Glass lenses did a great job on partly — and even mostly — cloudy days."Kyle Jaracz, Model Aviation, August 2024, reviewing Extreme Glare sunglasses
Where this comes from (10 sources, every claim re-checked 21 Aug 2026)
  • FAA, "Sunglasses for Pilots: Beyond the Image" (OK-24-1154, Sept 2024): gray recommended; gray, gray-green and brown all "an excellent choice"; yellow/amber "no scientific studies support" the sharper-vision claim; polarized "not recommended"; lenses blocking more than 85% of light not recommended. faa.gov
  • AOPA: traffic between you and a low sun is "very difficult to see."
  • Lab study, 2000 (Wolffsohn, Optometry & Vision Science): yellow lenses improved contrast of bright objects "against a blue-based background, such as the sky," at a cost to color vision. Brown was not tested.
  • Lab study, 2002 (de Fez): brown, green and blue lenses no different from gray for contrast; yellow/orange slightly better, worse for colors.
  • Two lab studies (Kelly, 1984 and 1990): people who had a preference chose yellow 2-to-1, and large scenes look up to 40% brighter through it, but they didn't actually see finer detail.
  • US Air Force field test (Aerospace Medical Research Lab, 1971): ten observers searching for ground targets in moderate haze; yellow sunglasses "of no value" for spotting them, and slightly worse for identifying them.
  • Physics papers (Applied Optics 2003, 2008): sky polarization peaks a quarter-turn from the sun; clouds and haze wipe it out.
  • American Academy of Ophthalmology: brown/rose give extra contrast; avoid polarized with screens; "polarization itself does not provide UV protection".
  • Zenni Optical: "dark (80% tint)" means 80% of light blocked, which is where the checklist's number comes from.
  • Pilot forums and press: HeliFreak, RC Universe, RCGroups, HobbySquawk, Model Aviation (Aug 2024), a soaring blog. RC Universe quotes came from search snippets; the threads themselves are behind a block.
