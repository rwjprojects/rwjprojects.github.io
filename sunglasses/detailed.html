Field handout for RC pilots · August 2026, updated September 2026

Which Sunglasses
for Which Sky

What polarization and lens color actually do when you're tracking a model against the sky — sorted by what's measured, what's official, and what's just the salesman talking.

Darkness matters more than color.

Gray or brown at 15–30 % light through (the dark end of category 2, the light end of category 3). Darker than that and the FAA warns you may see less detail. It's the FAA's clearest rule, though two FAA documents disagree on the exact limit (see the note under the color table).

Gray or brown: both work; neither is proven better.

Gray is the FAA's first recommendation. Brown matched gray for contrast in a lab test (2002), and pilots report it helps in haze. Yellow lenses measurably raised the contrast of a bright object against a blue background (lab study, 2000), at a cost to color judgment.

Skip polarized for flying.

It darkens clear blue sky only in a band 90° from the sun, does nothing under cloud and less in haze, can black out your transmitter screen, and the FAA says it masks the glint off a wing.

Blue lenses: no evidence. Yellow for overcast: weak.

No study shows blue tints help. Yellow makes flat light feel brighter (measured, up to 40 %) but doesn't reliably sharpen anything; rose hasn't been measured.

"Blink and it clears" is dry eye, not glasses.

Wind + sun + staring up with wide-open lids dries the tear film. Artificial tears, a wrap-style frame and a hat brim fix more than any tint will.

What polarization actually does

A polarized lens blocks light vibrating horizontally — the glare bouncing off water, roads, and car hoods. That's its whole job, and it's very good at it.

The sky is a different animal. Sunlight scattered by air is partly polarized — strongly (up to ~80 %) in a ring of sky 90° from the sun, and not at all when you look toward the sun or directly away from it. A polarized lens therefore darkens part of a clear sky and leaves the rest alone. Photographers use exactly this to make blue sky dramatic — and complain about the "ugly bands" it puts across wide shots.

Clouds and haze get in the way. Light from clouds is essentially unpolarized, so under overcast a polarized lens is just a dark lens. In haze, the particles weaken the effect without erasing it.

For us: on a clear day a light-colored plane gains some contrast while it's in the dark band, loses it elsewhere, and the sky brightness shifts under it as it crosses. On any other day, nothing. Meanwhile the lens can blank your transmitter's LCD at certain head angles and dims the sun-glint off a wing that many of us use to catch orientation.

"Polarized lenses are not recommended for use in the aviation environment. … polarization can reduce or eliminate the visibility of instruments that incorporate anti-glare filters … and mask the sparkle of light that reflects off shiny surfaces such as another aircraft's wing or windscreen."FAA, Sunglasses for Pilots: Beyond the Image (OK-24-1154, Sept 2024)
Polarized ≠ UV protection. "Polarization itself does not provide UV protection" (American Academy of Ophthalmology). UV400 / "100 % UVA-UVB" is a separate, usually free, box to tick — and it has nothing to do with how dark the lens is.

Categories are ISO 12312-1: cat 1 lets 43–80 % of light through, cat 2 18–43 %, cat 3 8–18 %, cat 4 3–8 % (not for driving). Online stores often quote tint darkness instead ("80 % tint") — that's roughly the opposite number, about 20 % through. The FAA's 15–30 % spans the dark end of category 2 and the light end of category 3, so a lens sold only as "category 3" can be darker than the FAA wants.

The FAA disagrees with itself on darkness. The 2024 pilot-sunglasses brochure says block "only 70 - 85%" of light and no more; the glare paragraph in the FAA's pilots' manual (AIM 8-1-6) says block "at least 85 percent". A lens that blocks about 85 % (15 % through) satisfies both. We follow the brochure: it is the FAA's dedicated sunglasses guidance. So 80 % is fine by the brochure; if a site offers 85 %, that meets both. The same AIM paragraph asks for lenses that pass "all colors equally" (neutral transmittance), which means gray.
Try it with your own plane. Your plane's colors matter too: a tint that makes one color scheme pop can wash out another. We found no test of lenses on model planes, so fly the same plane on the same day with each pair you own, and with none (swap pairs on the ground between flights, or have a buddy fly while you watch), and notice how soon you pick it up, whether you can tell which way it's pointing, and whether you can still read your transmitter.

Ordering checklist (any online optician)

  1. UV400 / 100 % UVA-UVB. Non-negotiable and usually free. Not related to darkness or polarization.
  2. Non-polarized. Save the upcharge. The one place it helps (clear blue, 90° from the sun) doesn't outweigh the radio screen and the wing glint.
  3. Color: gray, or brown/copper if you prefer it, for your main pair. Not blue.
  4. Darkness: around 70–85 % tint (15–30 % of light through). If the site only offers "80 %", take it. Don't go darker than 85 %.
  5. Your current distance prescription, from an exam in the last couple of years — squinting at a plane is a prescription problem first.
  6. Polycarbonate lenses — the FAA calls them "the most impact-resistant lenses available." Get a scratch-resistant coating (AAO: "Uncoated polycarbonate lenses tend to scratch easily"). Their Abbe value is 31 against 58 for standard CR-39 plastic, meaning more color fringing off-center (though CR-39 shows more of a different off-center blur); with a strong prescription, ask your optician about it. Our inference: in plain sunglasses it matters much less.
  7. Sunglasses aren't safety glasses. All US sunglasses must meet FDA impact standards (AAO), but near a running prop, safety glasses rated for high impact under ANSI/ISEA Z87.1 (current edition 2025; usually marked "Z87+") protect more. If you need a prescription, ask your optician for prescription safety glasses.
  8. A large, wrapped lens (or side shields). Blocks wind for your tear film and side glare for your eyes.
  9. Optional second pair for gray days: light yellow, 30–50 % tint (category 1). Cheap frame; it's for comfort in flat light, not sharpness. Rose is untested; try before you buy.
  10. In the field: hat brim, artificial tears in the flight box, and when the plane goes soft — blink hard, look at the tree line, look back.

Official

  • ISO 12312-1:2022 (standard; paywalled; Table 1 read from a reseller's (iTeh) preview) — category 2 lets through more than 18 % and up to 43 %; category 3 more than 8 % and up to 18 %.
  • FDA, Tips to stay safe in the sun — "Choose sunglasses with a UV400 rating or '100% UV protection' on the label"; "up to 80% of the sun's UV rays can get through the clouds."
  • FAA, Sunglasses for Pilots: Beyond the Image (OK-24-1154) — "Gray … is recommended because it distorts color the least. Some pilots, however, report that gray-green and brown tints … enhanc[e] contrast in hazy conditions." / "Yellow, amber, and orange … reportedly sharpen vision, although no scientific studies support this claim." / "Tints that block more than 85% of visible light are not recommended" / "sunglass lenses should screen out only 70 - 85% of visible light" / "Photochromic lenses are not recommended" / polycarbonate lenses are "the most impact-resistant lenses available" and "have a low Abbe value, indicating their inherent optical aberrations" (Abbe 31; CR-39 58; crown glass 59).
  • ISEA, ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2025 released (Jan 2026) — the current US eye- and face-protection standard. UCLA EHS, choosing safety glasses — "ANSI Z87+ is rated for high impact".
  • FAA AIM 8-1-6 (Change 3, July 2026; mirror) — empty-field myopia: eyes "seek a comfortable focal distance which may range from 10 to 30 feet." Glare: sunglasses that "absorb at least 85 percent of visible light (15 percent transmittance) and all colors equally" — a floor on darkness, which conflicts with the brochure's 85 % ceiling (see the note under the color table).
  • AOPA, The Scan — "be extra vigilant when the sun is low on the horizon. It makes any traffic between the observer and the sun very difficult to see."
  • US Air Force Aerospace Medical Research Lab (Heckart et al., 1971), AD730290, airborne reconnaissance with yellow sunglasses — "of no value as an aid to visual reconnaissance in an area search task under conditions of high ambient illumination and moderate haze" (NTRL abstract; detection 69% both groups, identification 55% vs 70%).

Peer-reviewed

  • Wolffsohn et al. 2000, Optom Vis Sci — tinted lenses give "a significant improvement … when measured using a white-on-blue grating … enhances contrast when viewing bright objects against a blue-based background, such as the sky", with "a systematic detriment to color vision".
  • de Fez et al. 2002, Optom Vis Sci — "Green, brown, and blue filters did not cause significant changes in contrast sensitivity compared with a gray filter of equal luminance, although chromatic discrimination was disturbed. Yellow and orange filters improved achromatic contrast at certain spatial frequencies, but impaired chromatic discrimination."
  • Doroslovački & Guyton 2015, J AAPOS — photographic simulation of off-axis blur from chromatic aberration in spectacle lenses: more in polycarbonate than in CR-39; CR-39 showed more oblique astigmatism (prescription lenses).
  • Kelly 1984 / 1990 — users preferred yellow 2:1 but "contrast thresholds … were not statistically different"; "brightness perception … up to 40% greater".
  • Hwang et al. 2019, JAMA Ophthalmol — yellow night-driving lenses "not better than with the clear lenses in any conditions."
  • Pust & Shaw 2008, Applied Optics — band of max polarization "90° from the Sun"; "light scattered from the cloud itself is effectively unpolarized."
  • Schechner et al. 2003, Applied Optics — in haze "a polarizing filter cannot remove the airlight on its own."
  • Sci Rep 2024 — ISO 12312-1 category table; low-UV sunglasses "may have a slight potential to increase hazards to the eye compared to not wearing sunglasses at all."

Eye health (professional bodies)

  • AAO — Sunglasses: "Brown or rose-colored lenses provide extra contrast"; "Polarization itself does not provide UV protection." Polarized lenses: avoid "When viewing LCD screens".
  • AAO — Dry eye: "Protect your eyes from drying wind by wearing wrap-around glasses outside." EyeWiki: concentration "reduce[s] the blinking rate"; wider lid opening in upward gaze increases exposure.
  • NEI/NIH — Dry eye: "Blurry vision"; "artificial tears". Cataracts: "Wear anti-glare sunglasses".
  • AAO exam intervals (summary): "55 to 64 years—every 1 to 3 years; and 65 years or older—every 1 to 2 years."

Trade and hobby (not evidence, but experience)

  • NSSF, shooting glasses for clay sports — bright sky: purple / copper / gray; overcast: yellow / light orange / rose.
  • HMANA (optician), sunglasses for hawk-watching — brown "highlight[s] objects (especially hawks in the sky)"; gray "will not give you any advantage as far as contrast".
  • 20/20 Magazine, the physics behind glare — "the sky itself is naturally polarized"; "Metals do not polarize reflected light in the same way."
  • Ralph Nordstrom, polarizing filters and blue skies — "facing the sun … no effect … until the sun is directly over your shoulder."

What we did not find: any study that measured detection of a small distant aircraft against the sky through different tints or polarized lenses. Everything above is physics, lab contrast tests, and experienced people's guidance — applied to our case. Treat it as well-founded advice, not proof.
